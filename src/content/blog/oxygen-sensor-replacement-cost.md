## Short answer

Most owners pay $145 to $405 to replace an oxygen sensor, parts and labor together. The sensor itself runs $80 to $250, labor runs $65 to $155, and the job usually takes about half an hour to just over an hour at a shop once the car is on the lift.

## What the oxygen sensor does

It measures how much oxygen is left in the exhaust and reports back many times a second, so the engine computer can keep the fuel mixture right. Most cars have at least two: an upstream sensor before the catalytic converter, which does the mixture work, and a downstream sensor after it, which checks that the converter is doing its job. When a quote names a sensor, ask which position it is for. The positions price differently, and "an oxygen sensor" without a position is an incomplete quote.

## Signs yours may be failing

- The check engine light is on, with a code pointing at a sensor or its heater circuit
- Fuel economy drops noticeably with no change in how you drive
- A rough idle or hesitation that comes and goes
- A failed emissions test
- The car runs normally but the light stays on (a lazy sensor often feels like nothing at all)

## Why the price range is wide

The part tier matters more than the labor on this job. A sensor from the company that supplies the factory part costs more than a budget unit, and sensors are a part where the cheaper unit can read inaccurately enough to bring the light back. Location matters too: an upstream sensor buried against the exhaust manifold takes longer to reach than a downstream sensor in open pipe, and a sensor that has heat-seized into the exhaust can turn a short job into a longer one.

One caution before you pay for a sensor: a sensor code does not always mean a dead sensor. The sensor reports what it sees, and an exhaust leak, a wiring fault, or a mixture problem upstream can make a healthy sensor report bad numbers. This is the same reason our [guide to code P0420](https://www.wrenchli.net/blog/check-engine-light-p0420) puts the oxygen sensor on the list of things to rule out before anyone replaces a catalytic converter. A shop should confirm the sensor itself has failed, electrically and by its readings, before quoting the part.

## Is it safe to keep driving?

Usually, for a short while. The car protects itself by running a default fuel mixture, which costs you fuel economy and, over time, extra strain on the catalytic converter. Treat it as a schedule-soon item, not a same-day emergency and not a ignore-it item. If the car is also running roughly or stalling, that changes the answer: have it looked at promptly.

## About these figures

They are Wrenchli editorial estimates for typical cars, built from typical parts prices and published labor times, and checked against more than one published source. The low end reflects an independent shop using a quality aftermarket sensor and the high end a dealer using the factory sensor. They are a planning ballpark, not a quote. Your vehicle's price can land outside the range, and a shop can only price your car after a hands-on look.
