## Short answer

Most owners pay $160 to $535 to replace a thermostat, parts and labor together. At an independent shop the job lands near the low end, around $160. At a franchised dealer it lands near the high end, around $535. The job usually takes 1 to 2 hours at a shop.

## What the thermostat does

It is the valve that controls coolant flow through the engine. Closed while the engine warms up, open once the engine reaches operating temperature, it holds the engine in the narrow temperature band where it runs cleanly and efficiently. When it fails, it usually fails in one of two directions, and the symptoms point opposite ways.

If it sticks closed, the engine overheats. The temperature gauge climbs, the heater may blow hot then cold, and coolant can boil over. That version is urgent: driving an overheating engine can warp parts that cost far more than a thermostat, so pull over safely, let the engine cool, and do not open the coolant cap while the system is hot.

If it sticks open, the engine runs too cold. The gauge sits low, cabin heat is weak, fuel economy drops, and the check engine light may come on. That version is rarely an emergency, but it is not a someday item either. An engine that never warms up wears faster and burns more fuel on every trip.

## Why quotes for this job vary so much

On most modern cars, the part a shop replaces is not a bare thermostat. It is the thermostat housing assembly, with the thermostat built into the housing. A quote priced on a bare thermostat is pricing a part your car may not even take as a separate piece, and it will grow when the shop opens the job up. When you compare quotes, ask whether each one is for the full housing assembly. That one question explains most of the spread.

The part itself runs $60 to $120 at an independent shop and $137 at a dealer, and the labor runs 1 to 2 hours. Access is the other variable: on some engines the housing sits right on top, and on others it hides behind other components that have to come off first.

## About these figures

The range runs from an independent shop using a quality aftermarket housing assembly (the low end) to a franchised dealer using the factory assembly at its list price (the high end), with labor at each channel's typical rates. The figures were rebuilt on November 14, 2026 from named parts sources (the assembly's street price at two major retailers and the genuine part's retail price) and checked against published labor times. They are a planning ballpark, not a quote, and your vehicle's price can land outside the range.
